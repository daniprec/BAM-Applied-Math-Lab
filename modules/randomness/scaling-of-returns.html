Scaling of Returns

Module 3: Modelling with Randomness

If log returns are independent draws from one stable law, their distribution at every time horizon is a rescaled copy of the one-step distribution. Mantegna and Stanley (1995) tested this idea on the S&P 500 index. This page states the prediction and their result.

The scaling prediction

Let the one-step log returns be i.i.d. symmetric stable with index \(\alpha\) and density \(p_1\). By Stable Distributions, the return over \(\Delta t\) steps, \(r(\Delta t)\), is stable with the same \(\alpha\) and a scale multiplied by \(\Delta t^{1/\alpha}\). Its density is

\[ p_{\Delta t}(x) = \Delta t^{-1/\alpha}\, p_1\!\left( x\, \Delta t^{-1/\alpha} \right). \tag{1}\]

At \(x = 0\) this gives the probability of return to the origin

\[ p_{\Delta t}(0) = p_1(0)\, \Delta t^{-1/\alpha} . \tag{2}\]

On log-log axes \(p_{\Delta t}(0)\) against \(\Delta t\) is a straight line with slope \(-1/\alpha\). For Gaussian returns \(\alpha = 2\) and the slope is \(-1/2\). The peak of the distribution is well sampled, so Equation 2 is easier to measure than the tail.

What Mantegna and Stanley found

Mantegna and Stanley (1995) used S&P 500 index values sampled every minute from 1984 to 1989. They report that:

  • \(p_{\Delta t}(0)\) follows Equation 2 for \(\Delta t\) from 1 to 1000 minutes, three orders of magnitude.
  • The slope is about \(-0.71\), which gives \(\alpha \approx 1.4\) (\(1.40 \pm 0.05\) in the paper). The exponent is stable over the six years of data.
  • After rescaling with Equation 1, the central part of the distributions at different \(\Delta t\) matches a Lévy stable law.
  • The far tails fall off faster than a Lévy law, approximately exponentially, so the variance is finite.

The last point led to the truncated Lévy flight: a Lévy flight whose largest steps are cut off. Sums of such steps look stable for a long range of \(n\) and only converge to a Gaussian after very many steps (Mantegna and Stanley 1994). Later work on the tails of individual US stocks found a power-law tail exponent close to 3, outside the stable range \(0 < \alpha \le 2\) (Gopikrishnan et al. 1998). This is consistent with the heavy-tail and aggregational Gaussianity facts of Stylized Facts of Returns.

A simulated check

The figure applies the \(p_{\Delta t}(0)\) method to two synthetic series of \(10^6\) i.i.d. returns: symmetric stable with \(\alpha = 1.4\) and Gaussian. The density at zero is estimated as the fraction of aggregated returns in a window \(|r| < \varepsilon/2\) divided by \(\varepsilon\), with \(\varepsilon\) equal to one tenth of the interquartile range at that horizon.

## A simulated check

The figure applies the $p_{\Delta t}(0)$ method to two synthetic series of $10^6$ i.i.d. returns: symmetric stable with $\alpha = 1.4$ and Gaussian. The density at zero is estimated as the fraction of aggregated returns in a window $|r| < \varepsilon/2$ divided by $\varepsilon$, with $\varepsilon$ equal to one tenth of the interquartile range at that horizon.

```{python}
#| label: fig-p0-scaling
#| fig-cap: "Estimated probability of return to the origin against horizon for i.i.d. stable ($\\alpha = 1.4$) and Gaussian returns. Lines have slopes $-1/1.4$ and $-1/2$."
#| echo: false

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np

from amlab.finance import aggregate_returns
from amlab.random_walks import sample_symmetric_stable


def p_zero(x):
    """Density at 0 estimated in a window of one tenth of the IQR."""
    q75, q25 = np.percentile(x, [75, 25])
    eps = 0.1 * (q75 - q25)
    return np.mean(np.abs(x) < eps / 2) / eps


rng = np.random.default_rng(24)
series = {
    "stable, $\\alpha = 1.4$": sample_symmetric_stable(1.4, 10**6, rng=rng),
    "Gaussian": rng.standard_normal(10**6),
}
horizons = np.array([1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500])

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 3.8))
for (label, r), slope, marker in zip(series.items(), [-1 / 1.4, -0.5], ["o", "s"]):
    p0 = np.array([p_zero(aggregate_returns(r, h)) for h in horizons])
    ax.loglog(horizons, p0, marker, label=label)
    ax.loglog(horizons, p0[0] * horizons**slope, "k-", lw=0.8)
ax.set_xlabel(r"horizon $\Delta t$")
ax.set_ylabel(r"$p_{\Delta t}(0)$")
ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

for label, r in series.items():
    p0 = np.array([p_zero(aggregate_returns(r, h)) for h in horizons])
    s_all = np.polyfit(np.log(horizons), np.log(p0), 1)[0]
    big = horizons >= 100
    s_big = np.polyfit(np.log(horizons[big]), np.log(p0[big]), 1)[0]
    name = label.replace("$", "").replace("\\alpha", "alpha")
    print(f"{name:18s} alpha (all) = {-1 / s_all:.2f}, alpha (dt >= 100) = {-1 / s_big:.2f}")
