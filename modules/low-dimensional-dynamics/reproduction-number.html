The Reproduction Number

Module 1: Low-Dimensional Dynamics

The basic reproduction number \(R_0\) is the average number of secondary infections caused by one infectious individual in a fully susceptible population (Hethcote 2000; Keeling and Rohani 2008).

R0 in the SIR model

In the SIR model of Compartmental Models, one infectious person infects susceptibles at rate \(\beta S \approx \beta\) when \(S \approx 1\), during an infectious period of mean length \(1/\gamma\). Hence

\[ R_0 = \frac{\beta}{\gamma} . \tag{1}\]

The threshold

The infectious equation is \(\dot I = \gamma I (R_0 S - 1)\). At the start of an outbreak \(S(0) \approx 1\), so

  • \(R_0 > 1\): \(I\) grows at rate \(\gamma(R_0 - 1) = \beta - \gamma\), and an epidemic occurs;
  • \(R_0 < 1\): \(I\) decays and the infection dies out.

This is the threshold theorem of Kermack and McKendrick (Kermack and McKendrick 1927; Hethcote 2000). During the epidemic, the relevant quantity is the effective reproduction number \(R_{\text{eff}}(t) = R_0 S(t)\). The number of infectious people peaks exactly when \(R_{\text{eff}} = 1\), that is when \(S = 1/R_0\).

The same threshold appears in the SIS model. The endemic equilibrium of \(\dot I = \beta I(1 - I) - \gamma I\) is

\[ I^* = 1 - \frac{1}{R_0}, \]

which exists and is stable only for \(R_0 > 1\) (Hethcote 2000).

Herd immunity

Suppose a fraction \(p\) of the population is immune before the outbreak, so \(S(0) = 1 - p\). An outbreak cannot start if \(R_0 (1 - p) < 1\). The critical immunization fraction is

\[ p_c = 1 - \frac{1}{R_0} . \tag{2}\]

Vaccinating more than \(p_c\) of the population protects the whole population, including those not vaccinated (Hethcote 2000; Keeling and Rohani 2008). The formula assumes random mixing and a vaccine that gives full, lasting immunity.

What R0 does not tell you

  • \(R_0\) depends on the disease and on the population: the same pathogen has different \(R_0\) values in different contact patterns (Hethcote 2000).
  • In the SIR model, only \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) together determine the shape of the outbreak. Early data, which grow like \(e^{(\beta - \gamma)t}\), determine \(\beta - \gamma\) but not \(R_0\). You will see the consequences in Predicting the Peak.
NoteExercise

A disease has \(\beta = 0.3\) per day and a mean infectious period of 10 days. Compute \(R_0\), the early growth rate, the doubling time of cases, and the critical vaccination coverage.

TipSolution

\(\gamma = 0.1\) per day, so \(R_0 = 3\). The early growth rate is \(\beta - \gamma = 0.2\) per day, giving a doubling time \(\ln 2 / 0.2 \approx 3.5\) days. The critical coverage is \(p_c = 1 - 1/3 \approx 0.67\).

```{python}
#| echo: false
#| label: fig-herd
#| fig-cap: "Critical immunization fraction p_c = 1 - 1/R0."
#| fig-width: 6
#| fig-height: 3.5
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np

r0 = np.linspace(1, 15, 300)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 3.5))
ax.plot(r0, 1 - 1 / r0)
ax.set_xlabel("R0")
ax.set_ylabel("p_c")
ax.set_ylim(0, 1)
ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```
