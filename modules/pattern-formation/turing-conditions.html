Turing Conditions

Module 4: Space and Pattern Formation

NoteReading
  • Compulsory: Turing (1952).
  • Recommended: Murray (2003), Chapter 2, Sections 2.1 to 2.6.

Turing (1952) showed that diffusion can destabilize a uniform state that is stable without diffusion. This is called diffusion-driven or Turing instability. This page derives the four conditions for it in the notation of Murray (2003, sec. 2.3).

The Question

Take the linearized system from Laplacian Eigenmodes. Each mode with wavenumber \(k\) grows like \(e^{\sigma t}\), where \(\sigma\) solves

\[ \det\left(\gamma J - k^2 D - \sigma I\right) = 0, \qquad D = \operatorname{diag}(1, d). \]

You want the uniform state to be:

  • stable to the flat mode \(k = 0\) (stable without diffusion), and
  • unstable to at least one mode with \(k \neq 0\).

Trace and Determinant of the Mode Matrix

For \(A(k^2) = \gamma J - k^2 D\),

\[ \operatorname{tr} A = \gamma (f_u + g_v) - (1 + d)k^2, \]

\[ h(k^2) \equiv \det A = d\,k^4 - \gamma\,(d f_u + g_v)\,k^2 + \gamma^2 (f_u g_v - f_v g_u). \tag{1}\]

Stability at \(k = 0\) requires \(\operatorname{tr} J < 0\) and \(\det J > 0\). Then \(\operatorname{tr} A < 0\) for every \(k\), so the only way to get \(\operatorname{Re}\sigma > 0\) is \(h(k^2) < 0\) for some \(k^2 > 0\), which gives one real positive \(\sigma\).

\(h\) is a quadratic in \(k^2\) with positive constant term. It becomes negative only if its minimum lies at \(k^2 > 0\) and is negative. The minimum is at

\[ k_m^2 = \frac{\gamma\,(d f_u + g_v)}{2d}, \qquad h_{\min} = \gamma^2\left[\det J - \frac{(d f_u + g_v)^2}{4d}\right]. \tag{2}\]

The Four Conditions

Collecting the requirements gives the Turing conditions (Murray 2003, sec. 2.3):

\[ \begin{aligned} &f_u + g_v < 0, \\ &f_u g_v - f_v g_u > 0, \\ &d f_u + g_v > 0, \\ &(d f_u + g_v)^2 - 4 d\,(f_u g_v - f_v g_u) > 0. \end{aligned} \tag{3}\]

All derivatives are evaluated at the steady state. The conditions do not contain \(\gamma\) or the domain size. They are necessary for patterns. On a finite domain you also need an admissible wavenumber inside the unstable band (see Dispersion Relation).

What the Signs Tell You

  • The first and third conditions force \(d \neq 1\). If both species diffuse equally, no Turing instability exists.
  • They also force \(f_u\) and \(g_v\) to have opposite signs. Call the species with \(f_u > 0\) the activator. Then \(g_v < 0\) and \(d > -g_v / f_u > 1\): the inhibitor must diffuse faster than the activator.
  • With \(f_u g_v < 0\), the second condition needs \(f_v g_u < 0\). Either the activator promotes the inhibitor and the inhibitor suppresses the activator (\(g_u > 0\), \(f_v < 0\)), or the opposite cross signs hold (Murray 2003, sec. 2.3).

The usual summary is local activation with long-range inhibition (Gierer and Meinhardt 1972; Kondo and Miura 2010).

The Critical Diffusion Ratio

At the onset \(h_{\min} = 0\), so the critical ratio \(d_c\) solves

\[ f_u^2\,d_c^2 + 2\,(2 f_v g_u - f_u g_v)\,d_c + g_v^2 = 0, \tag{4}\]

taking the root with \(d_c f_u + g_v > 0\) (Murray 2003, sec. 2.3). For \(d > d_c\) a band of wavenumbers is unstable.

Worked Example: Gierer-Meinhardt

From Linearization, \(f_u = b(1-a)/(1+a)\), \(f_v = -b^2/(1+a)^2\), \(g_u = 2(1+a)/b\), \(g_v = -1\), and \(\det J = b\). So \(f_u > 0\) needs \(a < 1\), and \(u\) is the activator. With \(a = 0.4\) and \(b = 1\):

from amlab.turing import critical_diffusion_ratio, gierer_meinhardt_jacobian, turing_conditions

fu, fv, gu, gv = gierer_meinhardt_jacobian(a=0.40, b=1.00)
print(f"f_u = {fu:.4f}, f_v = {fv:.4f}, g_u = {gu:.4f}, g_v = {gv:.4f}")
print(f"d_c = {critical_diffusion_ratio(fu, fv, gu, gv):.2f}")
for d in (20, 30):
    print(f"d = {d}: Turing conditions hold? {bool(turing_conditions(fu, fv, gu, gv, d))}")
f_u = 0.4286, f_v = -0.5102, g_u = 2.8000, g_v = -1.0000
d_c = 26.24
d = 20: Turing conditions hold? False
d = 30: Turing conditions hold? True

So \(d = 20\) gives no Turing instability and \(d = 30\) does. You will test this prediction in the laboratory.

NoteExercise

For Gierer-Meinhardt with \(b = 1\), show that the fourth condition reads \(d f_u - 1 > 2\sqrt{d}\), and solve it for \(\sqrt{d}\).

TipSolution

With \(g_v = -1\) and \(\det J = b = 1\), the third and fourth conditions together say \(d f_u - 1 > 2\sqrt{d}\). This is a quadratic inequality in \(s = \sqrt d\): \(f_u s^2 - 2s - 1 > 0\). For \(f_u > 0\) it holds for \(s > \left(1 + \sqrt{1 + f_u}\right)/f_u\). With \(a = 0.4\), \(f_u = 3/7\) and \(s > 5.122\), so \(d > 26.24\).
